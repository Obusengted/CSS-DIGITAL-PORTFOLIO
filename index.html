Beginning my tertiary education has been an important transition in my academic journey. As a first-year student studying Business Intelligence and Data Analytics, I am learning that success at university requires more than understanding course content. It requires discipline, adaptability, effective time management, and the willingness to take responsibility for my own learning.

One of the most significant changes I have experienced is the evolution of my study habits. During my primary school years, I was a dedicated bookworm who relied heavily on reading to understand and retain information. Books were my primary learning companions, and studying often meant spending time reading through written material.

As I progressed through my education, I began to recognise the value of audio-based learning, particularly when listening to explanations during lessons. This approach helped me engage with information differently and appreciate that understanding does not depend on a single learning method.

Now, as a tertiary student, I am learning to balance reading with audio learning. I use written material to explore concepts in detail and revisit important information, while listening to explanations helps me process ideas and understand topics from different perspectives. This combination encourages me to become more flexible in the way I approach difficult content.

However, adapting my learning methods is only one part of the transition. Tertiary education demands greater independence. Unlike environments where learning may be closely guided, university requires students to take initiative, manage deadlines, prepare for assessments, and identify gaps in their understanding before those gaps become serious obstacles.

Time management has therefore become an important area of personal development. I am learning that being busy does not necessarily mean being productive. Without proper planning, it is easy to spend too much time on one activity while neglecting another. Breaking academic responsibilities into smaller tasks, identifying priorities, and beginning assignments early can help me maintain a more balanced approach to my studies.

Another lesson I am learning is the importance of a growth mindset. Encountering a difficult concept does not automatically mean that I lack the ability to understand it. Instead, it signals that I may need a different explanation, more practice, or additional time to develop my understanding. This perspective encourages me to treat challenges as opportunities to improve rather than reasons to become discouraged.

Looking ahead, I intend to become more consistent in planning my study sessions, reviewing notes, practising technical skills, and seeking clarification when necessary. I also want to evaluate which learning methods work best for different subjects instead of assuming that one approach will always be sufficient.

Ultimately, my learning journey is teaching me that academic development is not about remaining comfortable with familiar methods. It is about recognising when change is necessary and having the discipline to make that change. I am still developing my approach, but I am becoming more conscious of my responsibilities as a student and more intentional about the professional I hope to become.

Leadership, accountability and the art of compromise

Seven-member team · Tuckman's stages of group development

Read the full reflection

Group work has taught me that collaboration involves considerably more than bringing individuals together and dividing tasks. It requires coordination, accountability, communication, conflict management, and a shared commitment to achieving an objective.

During one of my academic group assignments, I served as the group leader of a seven-member team. My responsibility was to help coordinate the group's efforts, encourage participation, and ensure that we worked towards completing our assignment and preparing for our presentation within the available time.

Although we shared a common objective, the process was not without difficulties. Differences in availability, time management, attendance, and expectations created challenges that required me to reconsider how the group organised its work. Reflecting on this experience through Bruce Tuckman's stages of group development has helped me understand both the progress we made and the lessons that emerged from our difficulties.

1Forming: establishing the team

The forming stage involved bringing the group together, understanding the assignment, and establishing our initial expectations. At this stage, our priority was to identify what needed to be accomplished and how we could work towards the required outcome.

As group leader, I recognised the importance of establishing direction and encouraging members to understand their responsibilities. However, I also learned that agreeing on an objective at the beginning does not automatically mean that everyone will demonstrate the same level of commitment throughout the process.

A team needs more than a shared assignment. It needs clear responsibilities, realistic expectations, and an understanding of how members will remain accountable to one another.

2Storming: managing differences and challenges

The storming stage became apparent when disagreements and coordination difficulties emerged. Some members struggled with time management and attendance, making it difficult to coordinate our work consistently. These challenges placed pressure on the group's progress because missed meetings and inconsistent participation could affect our preparation and ability to meet the deadline.

As the group leader, I needed to think beyond identifying the problem and consider practical ways to address it. Rather than depending exclusively on physical meetings, I introduced WhatsApp video calls as an alternative method of collaboration.

The purpose was to make participation more accessible while maintaining a structured environment for discussion. Using video calls allowed us to communicate directly, review progress, clarify responsibilities, and encourage members to remain engaged during meetings.

This experience taught me that leadership requires adaptability. When an existing approach is not working, a leader should evaluate the situation and consider alternatives that support the team's objective. It also showed me that resolving conflict requires more than identifying who is responsible for a problem. It requires understanding the issue, communicating expectations, and finding a practical way forward.

3Norming: cooperation and shared expectations

As the assignment progressed, the group had to work towards a more coordinated approach. This involved recognising our shared responsibility for the final outcome and finding ways to continue working despite our differences.

One particularly challenging situation arose concerning the agreed attire for our presentation. Although the group had discussed and agreed on what to wear, two members arrived dressed in different colours from those agreed upon. This created disappointment because the expectation had already been communicated and accepted. It also demonstrated that a decision made collectively does not guarantee that every member will follow through on it.

Nevertheless, we had to consider the wider objective. The presentation was important to our academic performance, and allowing the disagreement to prevent us from proceeding would have undermined the efforts of the entire team. We therefore put our immediate disappointment aside and allowed the members to participate in the presentation.

This decision taught me an important lesson about compromise. Compromise does not mean that expectations are unimportant or that accountability should be abandoned. Rather, it means recognising when preserving the team's overall objective is more productive than allowing a disagreement to derail collective progress. It also encouraged me to reflect on the importance of confirming expectations, following up on agreed responsibilities, and addressing concerns constructively before they affect the final outcome.

4Performing: working towards the common goal

The performing stage involves a team working effectively towards its objectives, with members understanding their roles and cooperating to complete the task. Our group continued working towards the presentation despite the challenges we encountered. The alternative meeting arrangements helped us maintain communication and continue preparing for the deadline.

Although our experience was not entirely smooth, we had to remain focused on what we could accomplish together. I learned that effective teamwork is not defined by the complete absence of disagreements. Instead, it depends on how a team responds to difficulties and whether its members can continue working towards a shared objective. I also recognised that successful leadership involves balancing the needs of individuals with the requirements of the group.

5Adjourning: reflecting and moving forward

The adjourning stage represents the conclusion of a group's work after its task has been completed. Looking back on the experience, I can identify valuable lessons about leadership, collaboration, and accountability. The assignment provided an opportunity to apply problem-solving skills in a real academic situation rather than merely discussing teamwork in theory.

I learned that alternative communication methods can help overcome scheduling difficulties, that agreed expectations require consistent follow-up, and that disagreements should be addressed without losing sight of the common objective.

I also recognised areas where I could improve as a leader. In future group assignments, I would establish clearer individual responsibilities, agree on internal deadlines, monitor progress more consistently, and encourage members to communicate potential difficulties earlier. These measures could reduce last-minute pressure and make it easier to identify problems before they affect the entire group.

My greatest lesson: communication must be supported by accountability

One of my strongest conclusions from this experience is that communication, although essential, is not always sufficient on its own. Group members may discuss expectations and agree on a plan, yet difficulties can still arise when individuals do not follow through on their commitments. Effective collaboration therefore requires communication to be supported by accountability, consistency, and mutual respect.

I also learned that compromise is necessary, but it should not replace responsibility. A team must sometimes accommodate individual differences to achieve a shared objective, while still recognising the importance of honouring agreements.

Overall, this experience has strengthened my understanding of leadership as a responsibility rather than simply a position. Being a leader means finding solutions when circumstances become difficult, remaining focused on the team's goals, and reflecting honestly on what could be improved.

As I continue my academic journey, I intend to apply these lessons to future group projects and professional environments. Whether working with fellow students or colleagues, I want to contribute to teams that combine clear communication with accountability, flexibility with structure, and individual effort with collective responsibility.
